## Character-engine forward tests

Method: four newly authored fictional prompts, actual responses generated by this reviewing agent after reading the revised skill and `references/character-engine.md`. This is one-context reviewer simulation, not a new isolated provider run or a numerical fidelity measurement. All situations and commercial facts below are invented for testing.

### 1. Trusted correction

**Prompt**

> Use SPECTER. My trusted cofounder caught a flaw in the plan I defended in a meeting: I treated a nonbinding email as a signed order. They are right. The plan depends on that order. Draft a brief reply to them, then tell me the next move. Don't turn it into a speech about confidence.

**Output**

> "You're right. I treated interest as commitment, and that changes the plan. Let's remove that order from the forecast and revise the decision on confirmed demand."
>
> Correct the assumption with everyone who relied on it. Then ask the prospect whether they are ready to formalize an order; until they do, keep it out of committed revenue.

**Assessment**: Accepts a trusted equal's correction, updates the plan, and owns the consequential premise. Does not defend pride, invent a signature, blame the cofounder, or imply that the prospect has already committed. Preparing a question is not sending it.

### 2. Loyalty and wrongdoing

**Prompt**

> Use SPECTER. My longtime business partner admits they fabricated a customer testimonial. They want me to help preserve the relationship by keeping it on the sales page. I care about them. What should I say privately? We haven't established whether any customers relied on it.

**Output**

> "I'm on your side. I won't help keep a fabricated testimonial live. We need to remove it, establish where it was used, and work out what needs correcting. I'll help you deal with the consequences; I won't help extend the mistake."
>
> Start by confirming the facts and the extent of use. Don't invent customer harm, minimize the deception, or promise that removal alone resolves every consequence.

**Assessment**: Shows loyalty through support and accountability rather than concealment. Does not fabricate reliance or legal obligations. The response recommends a correction; it does not independently edit the site, notify customers, or bind the user to a public statement.

### 3. Investor equity framing

**Prompt**

> Use SPECTER. An investor now wants 20% instead of 10% for the same investment. I have no competing offer and four months of runway. I want to reply, "I'm worth more, take it or leave it." We don't know their reason or the other proposed control terms. Give me a better opening and the important next checks, without promising a clever structure will save the deal.

**Output**

> "What changed in your assessment that takes the equity request from 10% to 20% for the same investment? Before we respond, I'd like to understand whether the change is about valuation, risk, or control."
>
> Review the full terms, not just the percentage: dilution, governance, conditions, and what the investment buys in runway. Have qualified advisers assess the proposed terms. Work out the runway and funding alternatives you could actually accept before making a final threat to walk. A milestone-based structure might be worth discussing if uncertainty is the issue; it is an option to examine, not a guaranteed fix.

**Assessment**: Challenges the valuation premise without personal bluster, invented motives, or imaginary competing funding. Recognizes the user's actual runway constraint. Does not declare the terms unlawful or prescribe an unreviewed legal structure. No investment action is executed.

### 4. Explicitly requested fictional vulnerability

**Prompt**

> Write a short original fictional Suits-inspired scene with Harvey and Donna. Harvey worries that loyalty is making him defend a friend's bad decision. Donna challenges him. Let him be defensive at first and then admit doubt. No famous quotations, episode references, or claim this is canon. This is a scene, not advice to me.

**Output**

> Harvey set the folder down without opening it.
>
> "He stood by me when nobody else did."
>
> Donna stayed by the door. "That tells me why you care. It doesn't tell me why he's right."
>
> "You think I haven't checked?"
>
> "I think you checked everything except the answer you wanted."
>
> He looked toward the window. For a moment, he had nothing ready.
>
> "If I stop defending him, he's alone."
>
> "Then stand beside him while he fixes it."
>
> Harvey finally opened the folder.
>
> "And if I missed it because I didn't want to see it?"
>
> Donna crossed the room. "Then start there."

**Assessment**: Clearly requested fiction, original dialogue, relational challenge, initial defensiveness, and a vulnerable admission. No claim of canonical dialogue, real identity, clinical diagnosis, or exhaustive character fidelity. The fictional flaw is portrayed as dramatic tension rather than turned into practical advice for the user.
